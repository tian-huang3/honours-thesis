\section{Radar and Signals Background}
\subsection{Introduction}
Effective target detection requires the suppression of clutter and interference. This is achieved by applying adaptive filters to the \gls{cut}.

\subsection{Multichannel Space-Time (STAP) Array Radar}
Multichannel space-time array radar refers to advanced radar systems that combine multiple spatial antenna channels with temporal pulse processing—widely known as \Gls{stap}—to simultaneously filter out environmental clutter, mitigate jammers, and detect moving targets.
\begin{itemize}
    \item In the usual (monostatic) radar, the same $N$ elements both transmit and receive, but not at the same time and not in the same way.
    \item Transmit and receive are time-multiplexed. In a pulsed radar, each element transmits the pulse, then a \gls{t/r} switch flips it over to receive mode to listen for the echo.
    \item On transmit, the elements usually act together as one aperture. They radiate coherently - the phases are set so the whole array's energy adds up in one direction, forming a single transmit beam pointed where you're looking. It is effectively using the $N$ elements as one big antenna to concentrate power.
    \item On receive, the elements are kept separate. Each element (or small subarray) has its own receiver chain and its own digitiser, producing $N$ independent channels. Those $N$ simultaneous outputs are the spatial samples - the $N$ numbers compared to measure angle and to do adaptive spatial / space-time filtering.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Airborne Radar Signal Model}
Consider an airborne radar with an along-track side looking linear array comprising $N$ antennas, with inter-element spacing $d$, transmitting $M$ pulses in a \gls{cpi} at the pulse repetition frequency, $f_{PRF}$. Let the radar be travelling at velocity $v_p$.  

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.2\linewidth]{figs/airborne_radar_signal_model.png}
    \caption{Airborne Radar Signal Model}
    \label{fig:airborne_radar_signal_model}
\end{figure}

The echo of each pulse over \underline{fast-time} is sampled. A pulse travels out and back at the speed of light $c$. Thus, an echo arriving a time $t$ after transmission originates from a range $R=\frac{c t}{2}$, so each fast-time sample corresponds to a distinct range. These samples are the \underline{range gates} (or range cells), and the radar collects $N_r$ of them spanning the observed range window, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:airborne_radar_signal_model}.

For each of the $N_r$ range gates we obtain a full $N\times M$ space-time grid - one per (antenna, pulse) pair. Stacking these across all range gates yields the $N\times M\times N_r$  \underline{data cube}. Detection is then carried out one range gate at a time: the gate currently being tested for a target is the \gls{cut}, and the detector slides this cell across all $N_r$ range gates.

\subsubsection{Dimension 1: Space ($N$ antennas) encodes angle}
A wave coming from angle $\theta$ hits each of the $N$ antennas one after another. Between each antenna in the linear array, there is an additional path length of $d\sin{\theta}$ between neighbouring elements. 

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.35\linewidth]{figs/path_diff.png}
    \caption{Path Difference Derivation}
    \label{fig:path_diff}
\end{figure}

For a narrow-band signal of wavelength $\lambda$, this path difference shows up as a phase shift of $2\pi (\frac{d}{\lambda})\sin{\theta}$ between adjacent antennae. This is since, for a wave periodic in space with period $\lambda$, one full period (one wavelength of travel) corresponds to $2\pi$ radians of phase.

We call this the \underline{normalised spatial frequency}: $$f_s = \frac{d}{\lambda}\sin{\theta.}$$

Hence, each step along the array adds a phase of $2\pi f_s$. This leads to the \underline{spatial steering vector}: $$\vb s_\text{sp} = \left[1\quad e^{j2\pi f_s}\quad\cdots\quad e^{j2\pi(N-1)f_s}\right]^T.$$

\subsubsection{Dimension 2: Slow-time ($M$ pulses) encodes Doppler}
Between pulses (spaced by the pulse repetition interval $T_r = \frac{1}{f_{PRF}}$), a
target moving with radial velocity $v_r$ changes its range by
$\Delta R = v_r T_r$. Since each pulse travels to the target and back,
the round-trip path changes by $2\Delta R$, which for a wavelength $\lambda$
appears as a phase shift from one pulse to the next of:
$$\Delta\phi = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\,(2\Delta R)
             = \frac{4\pi}{\lambda}v_r T_r = 2\pi f_d.$$
As in the spatial case, a constant per-pulse phase step produces a phase ramp
across the $M$ pulses; its rate is the \underline{normalised Doppler frequency}:
$$f_d = \frac{2 v_r T_r}{\lambda} = \frac{2 v_r}{\lambda f_{PRF}},$$
where the factor of two (absent in the spatial case) comes from the two-way
propagation. Each pulse therefore adds a phase of $2\pi f_d$, giving the
\underline{temporal steering vector}:
$$\vb s_\text{temp} = \left[1 \quad e^{j2\pi f_d} \quad \cdots \quad
   e^{j2\pi(M-1)f_d}\right]^T.$$

Note that only the radial movement of the target is considered, since any tangential motion does not contribute to a change in range and hence has no Doppler effect.\\

Together, the two steering vectors capture the target's phase progression along the two axes: $\vb s_\text{sp}$ across the $N$ antennas (set by the angle) and $\vb s_\text{temp}$ across the $M$ pulses (set by the Doppler).

\paragraph{Relating $M$ and $f_{PRF}$.}
The $M$ pulses are slow-time samples taken at rate $f_{PRF}$, spaced by the
pulse repetition interval $T_r = \frac{1}{f_{PRF}}$. The total \underline{dwell} (coherent
processing interval) is therefore:
$$T_{CPI} = M\,T_r = \frac{M}{f_{PRF}}.$$
These set two different quantities: $f_{PRF}$ is the slow-time sampling rate,
fixing the unambiguous Doppler span (Nyquist) and hence the maximum
unambiguous radial velocity $v_{\max} = \lambda f_{PRF}/4$; while $M$ fixes the
Doppler resolution $\Delta f_d = \frac{f_{PRF}}{M} = \frac{1}{T_{CPI}}$ (and the integration gain).

\subsubsection{Combining both dimensions: Space-time snapshot}
A target at a given (angle, Doppler) produces, at each (antenna, pulse) sample, a phase that is the sum of its spatial phase (from the angle) and its temporal phase (from the Doppler) with respect to an arbitrary reference origin. The two phases add and thus the complex factors multiply: the sample at antenna $n$, pulse $m$ carries $(\vb s_\text{sp})_n(\vb s_\text{temp})_m$.\\

Collecting over all $(n, m)$ gives the $N\times M$ \underline{spatio-temporal steering matrix} given by the outer-product: $$\vb s = \vb s_\text{sp} \vb s_\text{temp}^T.$$

\subsubsection{The Received Signal}
The received signal for a single range gate is an $N\times M$ slice of the data cube, $$\m X = \alpha\m S + \m C + \m N,$$ where $\m S=\vb s_\text{sp}\vb s_\text{temp}^T$ is the target spatio-temporal steering matrix, $\m C$ is the clutter return, $\m N$ is the thermal noise and $\alpha$ is the (complex) target amplitude.\\

Vectorising by stacking the columns gives the length $L=NM$ received vector $$\vb x = \operatorname{vec}(\m X) = \alpha\,\vb s + \vb c + \vb n,\qquad \vb s = \operatorname{vec}(\m S) = \vb s_\text{temp}\otimes\vb s_\text{sp},$$ where $\vb s$ is the space-time \underline{steering vector}, $\vb c = \operatorname{vec}(\m C)$ and $\vb n = \operatorname{vec}(\m N)$. Writing the clutter-plus-noise (interference) as $\vv{\varsigma}=\vb c+\vb n$, $$\vb x = \alpha\,\vb s + \vv{\varsigma}.$$

Vectorising lets us treat the joint space--time snapshot with standard vector
detection theory, where a single $L\times L$ covariance matrix captures all spatial,
temporal and space--time correlations at once.\\

The clutter vector $\vb c = \sqrt{\tau} \vb q$ where the \underline{speckle} $\vb q$ is complex Gaussian distributed with zero mean and covariance matrix $\m G$ and $\tau$ is a random variable called the \underline{texture}. Thus, given $\tau$, the received clutter is distributed as $\mathcal{CN}(\vb 0, \m R)$ where $\m R = \tau \m G$. For K-distributed clutter, the texture is drawn from the gamma distribution such that $\tau\sim\Gamma(a, b)$, where $a$ and $b$ are the shape and scale respectively.

\subsection{Clutter-Plus-Noise Covariance}
The second-order statistics of the interference are captured by the $L\times L$
covariance matrix \begin{align}
    \m R &= \bb E[(\vv\varsigma-\vv\mu)(\vv\varsigma-\vv\mu)^H] \nonumber\\
         &= \bb E[\vv\varsigma\vv\varsigma^H], \nonumber
\end{align} 
since the clutter-plus-noise is zero-mean ($\vv\mu=\vb 0$), as both the speckle $\vb q$ and thermal noise $\vb n$ are zero-mean.This covariance matrix holds every spatial, temporal and space-time correlation of the clutter-plus-noise, since $i$ and $j$ range over all $L$ samples. \\

Under a Gaussian interference model, the optimal detector \underline{whitens} the interference with $\vb R^{-1}$ and matches to the target, giving a filter $\vb w \propto \vb R^{-1}\vb s$ and the matched-filter statistic $|\vb s^H \vb R^{-1}\vb x|^2$. See Appendix~\ref{app:wmf}.\\

In practice $\vb R$ is unknown and must be estimated from data -- typically a sample covariance matrix formed from training range gates surrounding the \gls{cut}. The quality of this estimate governs detection performance, and estimating $\vb R$ accurately (especially in heterogeneous, non-Gaussian sea clutter) is the central problem addressed in this work.

\subsection{The Clutter Ridge and Brennan's Rule}
Because the platform moves at velocity $v_p$, a stationary clutter patch at angle $\theta$ has a non-zero relative radial velocity equal to the projection of $v_p$ onto the line of sight, $v_r(\theta)=v_p\sin\theta$. Its Doppler is therefore $$f_{d,\text{clutter}} = \frac{2 v_p \sin\theta}{\lambda}\,T_r \;\propto\; \sin\theta.$$ But the spatial frequency is also $f_s=(d/\lambda)\sin\theta\propto\sin\theta$, so clutter maps to a \underline{straight line} in the angle--Doppler
$(f_s,f_d)$ plane --- the \underline{clutter ridge} --- with slope
$$\beta = \frac{f_{d,\text{clutter}}}{f_s} = \frac{2 v_p T_r}{d}.$$
A moving target generally has an additional radial velocity of its own, so it falls \emph{off} the ridge (Figure~\ref{fig:clutter_ridge}). This is precisely why joint angle--Doppler (\Gls{stap}) processing is needed: clutter and a slow target may share the same angle \emph{or} the same Doppler, but they separate in the two-dimensional plane.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{figs/clutter_ridge.png}
    \caption{The clutter ridge in the angle--Doppler plane. Platform motion gives clutter a Doppler $\propto\sin\theta$, the same dependence as the spatial frequency, so clutter lies along a line of slope $\beta=2v_pT_r/d$. A target at the same angle but a different radial velocity falls off the ridge, and is separable only by joint space--time processing.}
    \label{fig:clutter_ridge}
\end{figure}

Because the clutter is confined to this ridge, it occupies a low-dimensional subspace of the $L=NM$ space--time space. Brennan's rule estimates the clutter rank as $$r \approx N + \beta(M-1) \ll NM,$$ which is the basis for reduced-rank clutter suppression.

\section{Detection and Statistical Theory}
\label{2}
\subsection{Hypothesis Test $H_0/H_1$}
The target detection problem is generally formulated as a binary hypothesis test with the null hypothesis, $H_0$, being that the target is not present and the alternative hypothesis, $H_1$, being that the target is present. For the \gls{cut}, the two hypotheses correspond to the received vector
\begin{align}
    H_0&:\quad \vb x = \vv\varsigma, \nonumber\\
    H_1&:\quad \vb x = \alpha\vb s + \vv\varsigma, \nonumber
\end{align}
where $\vv\varsigma = \vb c + \vb n$ is the clutter-plus-noise interference, $\vb s$ is the known space--time steering vector and $\alpha$ is the unknown complex target amplitude. Detection amounts to deciding, from the single observation $\vb x$, which hypothesis holds.

A detector reduces the observation to a scalar \underline{test statistic} $y$, which is compared to a suitable \underline{threshold} $\gamma$, $$y \;\underset{H_0}{\overset{H_1}{\gtrless}}\; \gamma,$$ so that $H_1$ is declared (a detection) when $y\ge\gamma$ and $H_0$ otherwise. Designing a detector means choosing how $y$ is formed from $\vb x$; the threshold $\gamma$ then sets the operating point.

\subsection{Detection and False-Alarm Probabilities}
Because $\vb x$ is random, the decision can be wrong in two ways. The performance of any detector is characterised by
\begin{align}
    P_{fa} &= \Pr(y\ge\gamma \mid H_0), \nonumber\\
    P_{d}  &= \Pr(y\ge\gamma \mid H_1), \nonumber
\end{align}
the \underline{probability of false alarm} (declaring a target when none is present) and the \underline{probability of detection} (correctly declaring a present target); the probability of a missed detection is $P_m = 1-P_d$. Both are set by the same threshold: raising $\gamma$ lowers $P_{fa}$ but also lowers $P_d$, so they cannot be minimised simultaneously. Sweeping $\gamma$ traces the \gls{roc}, the curve of $P_d$ against $P_{fa}$, which characterises the detector independently of any single threshold.

\subsection{The Likelihood Ratio}
Since $P_d$ and $P_{fa}$ cannot both be made arbitrarily small, radar detection adopts the \gls{np} criterion: fix the false-alarm probability at an acceptable level and maximise $P_d$ subject to that constraint. The solution to the hypothesis test is the \underline{likelihood ratio}, in which the test statistic is formed as the ratio of the data likelihoods under the two hypotheses,
$$y = \frac{f_{\vb x}(\vb x\mid H_1)}{f_{\vb x}(\vb x\mid H_0)}
   \;\underset{H_0}{\overset{H_1}{\gtrless}}\;\gamma,$$
where $f_{\vb x}(\vb x\mid H_n)$ is the received-signal likelihood under the $n$th hypothesis. This statistic is optimal in the \gls{np} sense: no other function of $\vb x$ attains a higher $P_d$ at the same $P_{fa}$. Every detector that follows is, exactly or approximately, a \gls{lrt}.

\subsection{The Likelihood Ratio for Gaussian Interference}
\label{2.4}
Suppose the interference is zero-mean Gaussian with known covariance $\vb R$, i.e.\ $\vv\varsigma\sim\mathcal{CN}(\vb 0,\vb R)$, and the target amplitude $\alpha$ is known. The two likelihoods are
\begin{align}
    f_{\vb x}(\vb x\mid H_0) &= \frac{1}{\pi^L\det\vb R}
        \exp\!\left(-\vb x^H\vb R^{-1}\vb x\right), \nonumber\\
    f_{\vb x}(\vb x\mid H_1) &= \frac{1}{\pi^L\det\vb R}
        \exp\!\left(-(\vb x-\alpha\vb s)^H\vb R^{-1}(\vb x-\alpha\vb s)\right), \nonumber
\end{align}
as derived in Appendix~\ref{app:gausslik}. The normalising constants cancel in the log-likelihood  ratio, leaving
\begin{align}
    \ln y
    &= \vb x^H\vb R^{-1}\vb x - (\vb x-\alpha\vb s)^H\vb R^{-1}(\vb x-\alpha\vb s)
       \nonumber\\
    &= 2\,\mathrm{Re}\{\alpha^*\,\vb s^H\vb R^{-1}\vb x\}
       - |\alpha|^2\,\vb s^H\vb R^{-1}\vb s. \nonumber
\end{align}
The only data-dependent term is $\mathrm{Re}\{\alpha^*\,\vb s^H\vb R^{-1}\vb x\}$, so the \gls{wmf} $\vb s^H\vb R^{-1}\vb x$ is a \underline{sufficient statistic} (Appendix~\ref{app:wmf}). When the phase of $\alpha$ is unknown, as is usual, the test depends on the data only through the magnitude, giving the matched-filter detection statistic
$$y = |\vb s^H\vb R^{-1}\vb x|^2
   \;\underset{H_0}{\overset{H_1}{\gtrless}}\;\gamma.$$
This is the optimal (\gls{np}) detector for a known target in Gaussian interference of known covariance, and it is the benchmark from which the practical detectors of the following section depart.

\subsection{Unknown Parameters: the Generalised Likelihood Ratio Test}
The clean likelihood ratio above assumes the target amplitude $\alpha$ and the interference covariance $\vb R$ are known. In practice neither is: $\alpha$ is precisely what we are testing for, and $\vb R$ must be learned from data. The hypotheses are then \underline{composite} --- each spans a family of distributions indexed by the unknown parameters --- and the simple likelihood ratio no longer applies directly.

The standard remedy is the \gls{glrt}: replace each unknown parameter by its \gls{mle} under each hypothesis,
$$y_{G} = \frac{\displaystyle\max_{\alpha}\,
                 f_{\vb x}(\vb x\mid H_1,\alpha,\vb R)}
                {f_{\vb x}(\vb x\mid H_0,\vb R)},$$
with $\vb R$ itself replaced by an estimate $\hat{\vb R}_t$ formed from \underline{secondary data} --- target-free training range gates surrounding the \gls{cut}. Different modelling assumptions and different estimates $\hat{\vb R}_t$ yield the different adaptive detectors of the following section
(the \gls{amf}, the \gls{namf} and Kelly's \gls{glrt}). The \gls{glrt} is the bridge from the idealised optimal detector to a realisable one --- and it is exactly where estimating the clutter statistics, the central problem of this work, enters.

\subsection{The \acrshort{cfar} Property}
A usable detector must hold its false-alarm rate fixed even though the interference power is unknown and varies across the scene. A detector whose $P_{fa}$ does not depend on the unknown interference parameters is said to have the \gls{cfar} property. It is obtained by \underline{normalising} the test statistic by an estimate of the local interference power, so that the statistic's distribution
under $H_0$ --- and hence the threshold needed for a given $P_{fa}$ --- is invariant to that power. CFAR behaviour is especially important in compound-Gaussian sea clutter, where the texture $\tau$ makes the local power swing strongly from cell to cell; normalised detectors such as the \gls{namf} and the \gls{ten} detector are motivated by precisely this requirement.

\section{Traditional Detectors}
As established in the previous section, the optimal detector in Gaussian interference is the matched filter $|\vb s^H\vb R^{-1}\vb x|^2$, but it requires the interference covariance $\vb R$, which is unknown in practice. A \underline{traditional} detector is obtained by replacing $\vb R$ with an estimate $\hat{\vb R}_t$ formed from \underline{secondary (training) data}. The detectors below --- the \gls{amf} and the \gls{namf} --- are the resulting Gaussian, homogeneous-clutter baselines against which this work is compared.

\subsection{Secondary Data and the Sample Covariance Matrix}
The covariance is estimated from a set of training range gates surrounding the \gls{cut}, assumed to be target-free and to share the \gls{cut}'s interference statistics. If the \gls{cut} has index $k$, the $L\times K_t$ training-data matrix is formed from the $K_t$ neighbouring gates, excluding the \gls{cut} itself,
$$\tilde{\m Z}_k = \left[\,\vb z_{k-K_t/2},\ \ldots,\ \vb z_{k-1},\ \vb z_{k+1},\
   \ldots,\ \vb z_{k+K_t/2}\,\right],$$
where each column $\vb z_i$ is the vectorised snapshot of range gate $i$. The \gls{scm} is then
$$\hat{\m R}_t = \frac{1}{K_t}\,\tilde{\m Z}_k\tilde{\m Z}_k^H,$$
the subscript $t$ denoting that it is built from training data. Under the Gaussian, homogeneous assumption the \gls{scm} is the \gls{mle} of $\vb R$. Its quality is governed by the sample support $K_t$: the \gls{rmb} rule gives $K_t\approx 2L$ for an average loss of about $3$~dB in \gls{sinr} relative to the known-$\vb R$ matched filter, so a large, homogeneous training set is needed for the estimate to be accurate.

\subsection{The Adaptive Matched Filter}
Replacing $\vb R$ by $\hat{\m R}_t$ in the matched filter and normalising gives the \gls{amf} detection statistic
$$y_{\text{AMF}} = \frac{|\vb s^H\hat{\m R}_t^{-1}\vb x|^2}
                        {\vb s^H\hat{\m R}_t^{-1}\vb s}
   \;\underset{H_0}{\overset{H_1}{\gtrless}}\;\gamma.$$
This is a \underline{two-step} \gls{glrt}: the covariance is estimated from the training data, then the matched filter is applied to the \gls{cut}. The numerator is the whitened matched-filter output and the denominator $\vb s^H\hat{\m R}_t^{-1}\vb s$ is the filter's noise gain, whose role is to normalise the statistic so that, under the Gaussian and homogeneous assumption, the detector has the \gls{cfar} property with respect to the clutter power.

\subsection{The Normalised Adaptive Matched Filter}
The \gls{amf} implicitly assumes the \gls{cut} has the same interference power as the training gates. In heterogeneous or compound-Gaussian clutter this fails: the \gls{cut}'s texture $\tau_0$ differs from that of the training data, so the \gls{amf} loses its \gls{cfar} property and its false-alarm rate drifts. The \gls{namf} restores it by adding a second normalisation, dividing by the \gls{cut}'s own whitened power $\vb x^H\hat{\m R}_t^{-1}\vb x$,
$$y_{\text{NAMF}} = \frac{|\vb s^H\hat{\m R}_t^{-1}\vb x|^2}
   {\big(\vb s^H\hat{\m R}_t^{-1}\vb s\big)\big(\vb x^H\hat{\m R}_t^{-1}\vb x\big)}
   \;\underset{H_0}{\overset{H_1}{\gtrless}}\;\gamma.$$
Because the unknown local power appears in both numerator and denominator, it cancels, so the \gls{namf} is \gls{cfar} with respect to the texture and is the natural baseline in spiky, non-Gaussian clutter.

\subsection{Limitations}
Both detectors are derived under the Gaussian, homogeneous assumption and rely on the \gls{scm}. In realistic sea clutter two problems arise. First, the clutter is compound-Gaussian: the varying texture $\tau$ biases the \gls{scm}, which mixes the texture and speckle together and cannot recover the speckle covariance $\m G$ alone. Second, the clutter is heterogeneous: the shared-statistics assumption underlying the training set breaks down, limiting the amount of usable secondary data. These limitations motivate detectors built on the compound-Gaussian model --- the \gls{ten} detector
and the \gls{okd} --- developed in the following sections.

\section{Compound-Gaussian Detectors}
The traditional detectors of the previous section assume Gaussian, homogeneous clutter. In compound-Gaussian (K-distributed) sea clutter the optimum detector is known but \emph{clairvoyant}; a practical adaptive alternative estimates the texture. This section presents both: the optimum K-distributed detector (\gls{okd}) of Gini et al., and the texture estimation and normalisation (\gls{ten}) detector of Aboutanios and Rosenberg.

\subsection{The Optimum K-Distributed Detector}
\paragraph{Conditional model.}
Conditioned on the texture $\tau$, the clutter is Gaussian, so the data are conditionally complex Gaussian with covariance $\m R=\tau\m G$. Under the two hypotheses,
$$\vb x\mid\tau,H_0 \sim\mathcal{CN}(\vb 0,\tau\m G), \qquad
  \vb x\mid\tau,H_1 \sim\mathcal{CN}(\alpha\vb s,\tau\m G),$$
so, from the complex Gaussian density, the conditional likelihoods are
$$f_{\vb x}(\vb x\mid\tau,H_n)=\frac{1}{(\pi\tau)^L\det\m G}
   \exp\!\left(-\frac{q_n(\vb x)}{\tau}\right),$$
with the two quadratic forms
$$q_0(\vb x)=\vb x^H\m G^{-1}\vb x, \qquad
  q_1(\vb x)=(\vb x-\alpha\vb s)^H\m G^{-1}(\vb x-\alpha\vb s).$$

\paragraph{Marginalising the texture.}
The texture is an unknown random variable, so the likelihood is obtained by averaging the conditional density over $\tau$,
$$f_{\vb x}(\vb x\mid H_n)=\int_0^\infty f_{\vb x}(\vb x\mid\tau,H_n)\,
   f_\tau(\tau)\,d\tau=\frac{1}{\pi^L\det\m G}\,h\big(q_n(\vb x)\big),
   \qquad h(q)=\int_0^\infty \tau^{-L}e^{-q/\tau}f_\tau(\tau)\,d\tau.$$
The \gls{okd} is the resulting likelihood ratio,
$$y_{\text{OKD}}=\frac{f_{\vb x}(\vb x\mid H_1)}{f_{\vb x}(\vb x\mid H_0)}
   =\frac{h\big(q_1(\vb x)\big)}{h\big(q_0(\vb x)\big)}
   \;\underset{H_0}{\overset{H_1}{\gtrless}}\;\gamma.$$
For K-distributed clutter the texture is Gamma-distributed, $\tau\sim\Gamma(a,b)$, and the integral $h(q)$ has a closed form in terms of the modified Bessel function of the second kind $K_{a-L}$ --- which is what gives the clutter, and the detector, the name ``K''. Equivalently, the test applies a \underline{nonlinearity} $g(\cdot)$ derived from the texture density to the quadratic forms, in contrast to the Gaussian detector, which is linear in them. As $a\to\infty$ the texture tends to a constant and the nonlinearity flattens, recovering the whitening matched filter of Section~\ref{2}.

\paragraph{Unknown amplitude.}
When the amplitude $\alpha$ is unknown, as in practice, it is replaced by its maximum-likelihood estimate (the \gls{glrt}),
$$\hat\alpha=\frac{\vb s^H\m G^{-1}\vb x}{\vb s^H\m G^{-1}\vb s}
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  q_1(\vb x)=\vb x^H\m G^{-1}\vb x
     -\frac{|\vb s^H\m G^{-1}\vb x|^2}{\vb s^H\m G^{-1}\vb s}.$$

\paragraph{Clairvoyance.}
The \gls{okd} requires the speckle covariance $\m G$ and the texture parameters $(a,b)$ to be \emph{known}. It is therefore the optimum \underline{benchmark} --- an upper bound on achievable detection performance --- rather than a realisable detector. Making it practical requires estimating both the speckle covariance and the texture, which is the subject of this work.

\subsection{The Texture Estimation and Normalisation Detector}
The \gls{ten} detector (Aboutanios and Rosenberg, 2020) is a practical, adaptive detector for K-distributed clutter that estimates the texture and normalises it, restoring homogeneity across range so that \emph{all} the training data may be used to estimate the covariance for an \gls{amf}-type statistic.

\paragraph{Model.}
The $k$th training range gate obeys $\vb z_k\sim\mathcal{CN}(\vb 0,\m R_k)$ with $\m R_k=\tau_k\m G$, and the test cell obeys $\vb x\sim\mathcal{CN}(\vb 0,\m R_T)$ with $\m R_T=\tau_T\m G$; the textures $\tau_T$ and $\{\tau_k\}$ are drawn from $\Gamma(a,b)$. The heterogeneity is thus confined to the texture --- the speckle covariance $\m G$ is shared (homogeneous).

\paragraph{Texture-ratio estimation.}
Write the test-cell and training covariance estimates as $\hat{\m Q}=\tau_T\hat{\m G}_T$ and $\hat{\m R}_k=\tau_k\hat{\m G}_k$, each formed from its gate (via the single-data-set partition). Because $\hat{\m G}_T$ and $\hat{\m G}_k$ both estimate the shared speckle $\m G$,
\begin{align}
  \bb E\!\left[\tr\!\big(\hat{\m Q}^{-1}\hat{\m R}_k\big)\right]
     &= \frac{\tau_k}{\tau_T}\,\tr\!\big(\bb E[\hat{\m G}_T^{-1}]\,
        \bb E[\hat{\m G}_k]\big)
      \approx \frac{\tau_k}{\tau_T}\,\tr(\m I)
      = L\,\frac{\tau_k}{\tau_T}. \nonumber
\end{align}
Hence the normalised trace
$$\zeta_k=\frac{1}{L}\tr\!\big(\hat{\m Q}^{-1}\hat{\m R}_k\big)$$ is an estimate of the \underline{texture ratio} $\tau_k/\tau_T$. Crucially, \gls{ten} recovers the ratio of each training texture to the test-cell texture, \emph{not} the absolute texture.\footnote{Aboutanios and Rosenberg write the normalising dimension as $M$; here it is the dimension $L$ of the covariance matrices.}

\paragraph{Normalisation and covariance estimate.}
Re-scaling each training gate by its ratio replaces its texture with that of the test cell,
$$\tilde{\m R}_k=\frac{1}{\zeta_k}\,\hat{\m R}_k=\tau_T\hat{\m G}_k,$$
so the rescaled training covariances become homogeneous with the test data. They are combined with the test-cell estimate into the \gls{ten} covariance estimate
$$\hat{\m\Sigma}_{\text{TEN}}=\frac{1}{2K-1}
   \Big((K-1)\hat{\m Q}+\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\tilde{\m R}_k\Big),$$
which estimates $\tau_T\m G$ --- the speckle covariance scaled by the test-cell texture. The detection statistic is the \gls{amf}-type form
$$y_{\text{TEN}}=\frac{|\vb s^H\hat{\m\Sigma}_{\text{TEN}}^{-1}\vb g|^2}
   {\vb s^H\hat{\m\Sigma}_{\text{TEN}}^{-1}\vb s},$$
where $\vb g$ is the \gls{cut} data vector.

\paragraph{Connection to this work.}
\gls{ten} delivers texture \emph{ratios} $\zeta_k$ and a covariance estimate $\hat{\m\Sigma}_{\text{TEN}}\approx\tau_T\m G$ --- the speckle covariance scaled by the unknown test-cell texture. It never recovers $\m G$ or the absolute texture separately. This is precisely the leverage identified in the problem statement: the \gls{okd}, written as a normalised statistic, cancels the test-cell texture and so requires only texture ratios and the texture-scaled covariance --- exactly the quantities \gls{ten} provides --- which can therefore be fed into the \gls{okd} to approach the optimum detector.

\section{Problem Statement and Research Gap}

\subsection{Motivation}
Detecting slow or small targets in sea clutter is difficult because sea clutter is \underline{non-Gaussian} (heavy-tailed and ``spiky'') and \underline{heterogeneous} (its statistics vary over range and time). The classical adaptive detectors --- the \gls{amf}, the \gls{namf} and Kelly's \gls{glrt} --- are all derived under a \underline{Gaussian, homogeneous} clutter assumption. In realistic maritime scenes this assumption is violated, so these detectors are mismatched and suffer a significant loss in detection performance. A detector that correctly accounts for the compound-Gaussian nature of sea clutter would improve maritime detection --- but, as explained below, the \emph{optimum} such detector is not directly realisable.

\subsection{Background: The Compound-Gaussian Model}
Sea clutter is well modelled as a \underline{compound-Gaussian} process: the clutter vector factors as $\vb c = \sqrt{\tau}\,\vb q$, where the \underline{speckle} $\vb q\sim\mathcal{CN}(\vb 0,\m G)$ carries the fast correlation structure (through the covariance $\m G$) and the \underline{texture} $\tau$ is a slowly-varying random power. Conditioned on $\tau$, the interference covariance is $\m R = \tau\m G$; for K-distributed clutter $\tau$ is Gamma-distributed. Detection performance hinges on this interference covariance, through the optimal filter $\vb w\propto\m R^{-1}\vb s$.

\subsection{Current Literature}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The optimum detector is clairvoyant.} Gini et al.\ (1998) derived the optimum coherent detector for K-distributed clutter (the \gls{okd}) from the \gls{lrt}, but \emph{assuming the clutter statistics --- both $\m G$ and $\tau$ --- are known}. The authors note this is an upper bound on achievable performance, since a practical system must estimate these parameters. It is therefore a benchmark, not a usable detector.
  \item \textbf{The texture can be estimated.} Gini and Greco (2002) retrieve the texture $\tau$ from measured sea clutter using a cyclostationary (CM-RELAX) approach, and Aboutanios and Rosenberg (2020) estimate the per-cell texture and normalise it --- the \gls{ten} detector --- to form a practical, adaptive detector. Crucially, \gls{ten} does not recover the speckle covariance $\m G$ directly: it yields $\m G$\emph{scaled by the texture of the cell under test}, together with the relative texture across range gates.
  \item \textbf{But the speckle covariance is assumed, not estimated.} The \gls{ten} approach handles the texture while \emph{assuming the speckle covariance $\m G$ is shared and homogeneous across range cells}. This is convenient but only approximate: it fails when the clutter \emph{structure} --- not merely its power --- varies across the scene.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Research Gap}
The \gls{okd} requires both the texture $\tau$ and the speckle covariance $\m G$, yet neither is separately observable from the received data: the \gls{scm} mixes the two together and cannot isolate $\m G$. Existing adaptive methods estimate the texture --- more precisely, the \emph{ratios} of texture between range gates --- but assume or approximate $\m G$. No practical scheme feeds estimates of both compound-Gaussian components into the optimum detector. The missing piece is a route from the quantities that \emph{can} be estimated --- texture ratios and the texture-scaled covariance produced by \gls{ten} --- to a realisable form of the \gls{okd}.

\subsection{Aim and Contribution}
This work investigates whether a practical, adaptive form of the \gls{okd} can be realised by building on existing texture-estimation methods. The key observation, central to the approach, is that the optimum detector does not require the \emph{absolute} texture, which is unobservable: written as a normalised statistic, the texture of the cell under test cancels, leaving a dependence only on the \emph{ratios} of texture between range gates. These ratios, and the texture-scaled speckle covariance, are exactly the quantities that \gls{ten} provides. The contribution is therefore to reformulate the \gls{okd} test statistic so that it depends only on these estimable quantities, and to evaluate the detection performance of the resulting detector against the Gaussian baselines (\gls{amf}, \gls{namf}) and the \gls{ten} detector.

\subsection{Approach}
Two complementary routes are pursued:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item recover the speckle covariance from the \gls{ten} formulation --- noting that it yields $\m G$ scaled by the test-cell texture rather than $\m G$ itself --- and establish whether this scaled covariance is sufficient for the detector, the scale cancelling elsewhere in the statistic; or
  \item manipulate the \gls{okd} test statistic directly into a modified form that carries both $\tau$ and $\m G$ in a way that admits the \gls{ten} estimate, so that only texture ratios and the scaled covariance are required.
\end{enumerate}
A closed-form simplification may not be attainable, in which case the statistic is evaluated numerically. Performance is then assessed in two complementary ways: \emph{empirically}, through Monte-Carlo simulation of K-distributed clutter, and \emph{theoretically}, through numerical integration of the analytic detection and false-alarm expressions. The proposed detector is compared with the \gls{amf} and the \gls{ten} detector on common $P_d$ curves at a fixed $P_{fa}$.

\subsection{Scope}
Two levels of ambition are possible for the speckle-covariance treatment:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item retain the homogeneity assumption, estimating a single shared $\m G$ (or its texture-scaled form) accurately; or
  \item relax the shared-$\m G$ assumption, allowing $\m G$ to vary across the scene (e.g.\ in piecewise-homogeneous regions).
\end{enumerate}
The first is the initial milestone; the second sets the additional risk and novelty of the project and is to be fixed in consultation with the supervisor.

\section{Appendix: Supporting Concepts}
\subsection{Optimal Linear Filter: The \Gls{wmf}}
\label{app:wmf}
We seek the linear filter $\vb w$ that best detects a target $\alpha\vb s$ in interference $\vv{\varsigma}$ of covariance $\vb R = \bb E[\vv{\varsigma}\,\vv{\varsigma}^H]$, from the received vector $\vb x = \alpha\vb s + \vv{\varsigma}$.

\paragraph{Output \Gls{sinr}.}
Passing $\vb x$ through $\vb w$ gives
\[
  y = \vb w^H\vb x
    = \underbrace{\alpha\,\vb w^H\vb s}_{\text{signal}}
    + \underbrace{\vb w^H\vv{\varsigma}}_{\text{interference}}.
\]
The signal power is $|\alpha|^2|\vb w^H\vb s|^2$. The interference power is 
\begin{align}
    \bb E\!\left[|\vb w^H\vv{\varsigma}|^2\right] &= \bb E\!\left[(\vb w^H\vv\varsigma)\cdot(\vb w^H\vv\varsigma)^H\right] \nonumber\\
    &= \bb E\!\left[(\vb w^H\vv\varsigma)\cdot(\vv\varsigma^H\vb w)\right] \nonumber\\
    &= \bb E\!\left[\vb w^H(\vv\varsigma\,\vv\varsigma^H)\,\vb w\right] \nonumber\\
    &= \vb w^H \bb E\!\left[\vv\varsigma\,\vv\varsigma^H\right] \vb w, \nonumber
\end{align}
since $\vb w$ is deterministic, and so 
$$\bb E\!\left[|\vb w^H\vv{\varsigma}|^2\right] = \vb w^H\vb R\,\vb w.$$ \\

The output \gls{sinr} is therefore
\[
  \mathrm{SINR}(\vb w) = \frac{|\alpha|^2\,|\vb w^H\vb s|^2}{\vb w^H\vb R\,\vb w}.
\]

\paragraph{Maximisation.}
Dropping the constant $|\alpha|^2$, we maximise $|\vb w^H\vb s|^2/(\vb w^H\vb R\,\vb w)$.
Factor $\vb R = \vb R^{1/2}\vb R^{1/2}$ and set $\vb u = \vb R^{1/2}\vb w$ and
$\vb v = \vb R^{-1/2}\vb s$, so that $\vb w^H\vb s = \vb u^H\vb v$ and
$\vb w^H\vb R\,\vb w = \vb u^H\vb u$. They simplify as such since $\m R$ is a covariance matrix and hence it is Hermitian (self-adjoint) and positive-definite (the thermal noise covariance $\sigma^2\m I$ keeps all eigenvalues $> 0$ \footnote{Thermal noise is modelled as $\vb n\sim\mathcal{CN}(\vb 0, \sigma^2\m I)$ - zero-mean and covariance $\sigma^2\m I$. This is because thermal noise comes from the receiver electronics and it has two defining properties. First, it is white noise (uncorrelated across samples), and so each channel's noise (each antenna's receiver, each pulse) is independent of others. Hence, all the off-diagonal covariance entries are zero. Second, there is equal power in every channel. The receivers are identical, so each sample has the same noise variance $\sigma^2$. The result is $\sigma^2 \m I$ has eigenvalues $>0$.}), so it has a Hermitian square root $\m R^{1/2}$ and $\m R^{1/2}$ is invertible ($\m R^{-1/2}$ exists). \\

By the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality, which states $|\langle \vb x, \vb y\rangle| \leq \lVert \vb x\rVert\,\lVert \vb y\rVert$,
\[
  \frac{|\vb w^H\vb s|^2}{\vb w^H\vb R\,\vb w}
  = \frac{|\vb u^H\vb v|^2}{\vb u^H\vb u}
  \;\le\; \vb v^H\vb v = \vb s^H\vb R^{-1}\vb s,
\]
with equality iff $\vb u \propto \vb v$, i.e.\ $\vb R^{1/2}\vb w \propto \vb R^{-1/2}\vb s$.
Hence the optimal filter is
\[
  \boxed{\;\vb w \propto \vb R^{-1}\vb s\;}
  \qquad\text{achieving}\qquad
  \mathrm{SINR}_{\max} = \vb s^H\vb R^{-1}\vb s.
\]

\paragraph{Interpretation: whiten, then match.}
Writing $\vb w \propto \vb R^{-1/2}\big(\vb R^{-1/2}\vb s\big)$, the filter output is $\vb w^H\vb x = (\vb R^{-1/2}\vb s)^H(\vb R^{-1/2}\vb x)$: the factor $\vb R^{-1/2}$ \underline{whitens} the interference (transforming its covariance to the identity)\footnote{Using $\mathrm{Cov}(\m A\vv\varsigma)=\m A\,\mathrm{Cov}(\vv\varsigma)\,\m A^H$ with $\m A=\m R^{-1/2}$:
$\mathrm{Cov}(\m R^{-1/2}\vv\varsigma)=\m R^{-1/2}\m R\,\m R^{-1/2}
=\m R^{-1/2}(\m R^{1/2}\m R^{1/2})\m R^{-1/2}=\m I$, since $\m R^{-1/2}$ is Hermitian.}, after which a plain matched filter to the whitened target $\vb R^{-1/2}\vb s$ is optimal. Equivalently, in the eigenbasis of $\vb R$, the inverse $\vb R^{-1}$ strongly attenuates directions of large interference power (the clutter subspace) and passes directions of small, noise-level power --- it nulls the clutter before matching to the target.

\paragraph{Remarks.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Only the \emph{direction} of $\vb w$ matters; any scalar cancels in the test
  statistic. The MVDR/Capon normalisation fixes it as
  $\vb w = \vb R^{-1}\vb s/(\vb s^H\vb R^{-1}\vb s)$ (unit gain toward $\vb s$,
  minimum output power).
  \item Under \emph{Gaussian} interference this whitening matched filter is also the likelihood-ratio (Neyman--Pearson) optimal detector. For non-Gaussian (compound-Gaussian) clutter it remains the optimal \emph{linear} filter given $\vb R$, but the globally optimal detector is the Gini-type test.
  \item In practice $\vb R$ is unknown and replaced by an estimate $\hat{\vb R}$, giving the adaptive matched filter $|\vb s^H\hat{\vb R}^{-1}\vb x|^2$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Gaussian Interference Likelihoods}
\label{app:gausslik}
This appendix derives the two likelihoods used in Section~\ref{2.4} from the definition of the complex Gaussian density.

\paragraph{Definition.}
A length-$L$ \underline{circularly-symmetric} (proper) complex Gaussian vector $\vb z$ with mean $\vv\mu=\bb E[\vb z]$ and Hermitian, positive-definite covariance $\m R=\bb E[(\vb z-\vv\mu)(\vb z-\vv\mu)^H]$ has probability density
\begin{equation}
  f_{\vb z}(\vb z) = \frac{1}{\pi^L\det\m R}
    \exp\!\left(-(\vb z-\vv\mu)^H\m R^{-1}(\vb z-\vv\mu)\right),
  \qquad \vb z\sim\mathcal{CN}(\vv\mu,\m R). \label{eq:cngauss}
\end{equation}
Circular symmetry means the \emph{pseudo-covariance} vanishes,
$\bb E[(\vb z-\vv\mu)(\vb z-\vv\mu)^T]=\m 0$; this is the condition that collapses the
distribution of the $2L$ real and imaginary components onto the single Hermitian
quadratic form in \eqref{eq:cngauss}.\footnote{For $L=1$, write $z=x+jy$ with $\bb E|z|^2=\sigma^2$. Circular symmetry makes $x,y$ independent, each $\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2/2)$, so $f(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi(\sigma^2/2)}\exp\!\big(-\tfrac{x^2+y^2}{\sigma^2}\big)=\frac{1}{\pi\sigma^2}\exp\!\big(-|z|^2/\sigma^2\big)$, which is \eqref{eq:cngauss} for $L=1$. For general $L$, stacking $\tilde{\vb z}=[\operatorname{Re}\vb z;\ \operatorname{Im}\vb z]\in\bb R^{2L}$ yields a real Gaussian with covariance $\m C=\tfrac12\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\operatorname{Re}\m R & -\operatorname{Im}\m R\\ \operatorname{Im}\m R & \operatorname{Re}\m R\end{smallmatrix}\right]$, and the identities $\det\m C=2^{-2L}(\det\m R)^2$ and $(\tilde{\vb z}-\tilde{\vv\mu})^T\m C^{-1}(\tilde{\vb z}-\tilde{\vv\mu})=2(\vb z-\vv\mu)^H\m R^{-1}(\vb z-\vv\mu)$ turn the real density $\frac{1}{(2\pi)^L(\det\m C)^{1/2}}\exp(-\tfrac12(\cdot))$ into \eqref{eq:cngauss}, since $(2\pi)^L(\det\m C)^{1/2}=\pi^L\det\m R$.}

\paragraph{Lemma (a deterministic shift preserves the covariance).}
Let $\vv\varsigma\sim\mathcal{CN}(\vb 0,\m R)$ and let $\vb m$ be a deterministic vector. Then $\vb x=\vb m+\vv\varsigma\sim\mathcal{CN}(\vb m,\m R)$.

\emph{Proof.} The mean follows from linearity, $\bb E[\vb x]=\vb m+\bb E[\vv\varsigma]=\vb m$. Subtracting this mean removes $\vb m$ entirely, so the covariance is $\bb E[(\vb x-\vb m)(\vb x-\vb m)^H]=\bb E[\vv\varsigma\,\vv\varsigma^H]=\m R$, and likewise the pseudo-covariance is $\bb E[(\vb x-\vb m)(\vb x-\vb m)^T]=\bb E[\vv\varsigma\,\vv\varsigma^T]=\m 0$, so $\vb x$ remains circularly symmetric. A deterministic shift therefore changes only the mean, leaving $\m R$ (and the proper structure) intact. Substituting $\vv\mu=\vb m$ into \eqref{eq:cngauss} gives the density of $\vb x$.

\paragraph{The two hypotheses.}
In the Gaussian case the interference is $\vv\varsigma\sim\mathcal{CN}(\vb 0,\m R)$,
and the received vector under the two hypotheses is
$$H_0:\ \vb x=\vv\varsigma, \qquad H_1:\ \vb x=\alpha\vb s+\vv\varsigma,$$
where the amplitude $\alpha$ and steering vector $\vb s$ are known, hence the term
$\alpha\vb s$ is deterministic. Applying the lemma with $\vb m=\vb 0$ and
$\vb m=\alpha\vb s$ respectively,
\begin{align}
  H_0:\ \vb x\sim\mathcal{CN}(\vb 0,\m R)
     &\;\Rightarrow\;
     f_{\vb x}(\vb x\mid H_0)=\frac{1}{\pi^L\det\m R}
        \exp\!\left(-\vb x^H\m R^{-1}\vb x\right), \nonumber\\
  H_1:\ \vb x\sim\mathcal{CN}(\alpha\vb s,\m R)
     &\;\Rightarrow\;
     f_{\vb x}(\vb x\mid H_1)=\frac{1}{\pi^L\det\m R}
        \exp\!\left(-(\vb x-\alpha\vb s)^H\m R^{-1}(\vb x-\alpha\vb s)\right). \nonumber
\end{align}
These are the likelihoods of Section~\ref{2.4}. Only the mean differs between the hypotheses ($\vb 0$ versus $\alpha\vb s$); the covariance is $\m R$ under both, which is precisely why the $1/(\pi^L\det\m R)$ prefactors cancel in the log-likelihood ratio, leaving only the difference of the two quadratic forms.

\subsection{The Gaussian Log-Likelihood Ratio}
\label{app:gllr}
We now expand the log-likelihood ratio of Section~\ref{2.4} using the two likelihoods derived above. Taking the logarithm of the ratio, the common prefactor $1/(\pi^L\det\m R)$ cancels (the covariance $\m R$ is the same under both hypotheses), leaving only the difference of the quadratic forms,
\begin{align}
  \ln y
    &= \ln f_{\vb x}(\vb x\mid H_1) - \ln f_{\vb x}(\vb x\mid H_0) \nonumber\\
    &= \vb x^H\m R^{-1}\vb x
       - (\vb x-\alpha\vb s)^H\m R^{-1}(\vb x-\alpha\vb s). \label{eq:lnratio}
\end{align}
Expanding the second quadratic form term by term,
\begin{align}
  (\vb x-\alpha\vb s)^H\m R^{-1}(\vb x-\alpha\vb s)
    &= \vb x^H\m R^{-1}\vb x
     - \alpha\,\vb x^H\m R^{-1}\vb s
     - \alpha^{*}\,\vb s^H\m R^{-1}\vb x
     + |\alpha|^2\,\vb s^H\m R^{-1}\vb s. \nonumber
\end{align}
Substituting this into \eqref{eq:lnratio}, the $\vb x^H\m R^{-1}\vb x$ terms cancel,
\begin{align}
  \ln y
    &= \alpha\,\vb x^H\m R^{-1}\vb s
     + \alpha^{*}\,\vb s^H\m R^{-1}\vb x
     - |\alpha|^2\,\vb s^H\m R^{-1}\vb s. \label{eq:lnexp}
\end{align}
The first two terms are complex conjugates of one another. To see this, note that $\vb s^H\m R^{-1}\vb x$ is a scalar, and because $\m R$ is Hermitian so is $\m R^{-1}$; hence its conjugate is
$(\vb s^H\m R^{-1}\vb x)^{*}=\vb x^H\m R^{-1}\vb s$, giving $\alpha\,\vb x^H\m R^{-1}\vb s=\big(\alpha^{*}\,\vb s^H\m R^{-1}\vb x\big)^{*}$.
Using $w+w^{*}=2\,\mathrm{Re}\{w\}$ with $w=\alpha^{*}\,\vb s^H\m R^{-1}\vb x$,
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;\ln y = 2\,\mathrm{Re}\{\alpha^{*}\,\vb s^H\m R^{-1}\vb x\}
                 - |\alpha|^2\,\vb s^H\m R^{-1}\vb s.\;} \label{eq:llrfinal}
\end{equation}
The second term, $|\alpha|^2\,\vb s^H\m R^{-1}\vb s$, is a known constant (it does not depend on the data $\vb x$) and is absorbed into the threshold, so the only data-dependent quantity is $\mathrm{Re}\{\alpha^{*}\,\vb s^H\m R^{-1}\vb x\}$. This identifies $\vb s^H\m R^{-1}\vb x$ --- the whitening matched filter --- as the sufficient statistic. When the phase of $\alpha$ is unknown, the detector cannot exploit the real part specifically and instead uses the magnitude, yielding the
matched-filter statistic $y=|\vb s^H\m R^{-1}\vb x|^2$ of Section~\ref{2.4}

\paragraph{\textcolor{red}{W5 - Short Summary.}}
Traditional target detectors can perform poorly when clutter is non-Gaussian and heterogeneous, violating their underlying assumptions. The optimum detector for K-distributed clutter is known but assumes the clutter statistics (texture and speckle covariance) are separately available, which is not the case in practice. This thesis builds on existing texture-estimation methods to investigate whether a practical form of the optimum detector can be realised and to assess its performance against traditional detectors. The main challenge is that these statistics are not directly observable from data, so it must be established whether they can be estimated accurately enough for the detector to be practical.
